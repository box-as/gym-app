The Consolidated Hypertrophy Program

5 Days/Week · Upper/Lower/Push/Pull · 4-Block Rotation (44 Weeks)

This combines Program 1's progression system, RIR targets, and nutrition framework with Program 3/4's smarter exercise selection (natural squat stance, trimmed overlapping isolation volume, flexible equipment choices).

The Rules (read this before you start)

Progression — Double Progression Stay at the same weight until you hit the top of the rep range on all working sets. Then add the smallest possible jump (2.5 kg / 5 lb, or one pin/plate step on cables) and drop back to the bottom of the range.

Choosing a starting weight: in Week 1, pick a load where your first working set lands inside the rep range at the target RIR, and adjust over the first session or two. If you finish with reps to spare, go heavier next time.

Lifting straps: use them on Friday's Barbell RDL and on Thursday's shrugs, so grip and forearm fatigue from Thursday never ends a hamstring set early. On Thursday's rows and pulldowns, use them whenever your grip gives out before your back does.

Effort (RIR = Reps In Reserve)

  • Compound lifts: stop 1–2 reps short of failure
  • Isolation lifts: stop 0–1 reps short of failure
  • The last set of every isolation exercise: true failure, safe machines/cables only (except Monday's rear-delt fly and Friday's lateral raise, which stop at 1 RIR with no failure set — they're the 2nd/3rd weekly exposure for those muscles)

Warm-up 5 min easy cardio, then on the first exercise of the day: 2–3 ramp-up sets (\~50%, 70%, 85% of working weight, for 5-3-1 reps). Every other exercise: one light set before working sets.

Tempo 1–2 second controlled lowering, full stretch, no bouncing, full range of motion every rep.

Log every set. Weight × reps, every exercise, every session. If a lift hasn't moved in 3–4 weeks, fix sleep/food first — don't just swap the exercise.

Session length: \~60–75 minutes.

WEEK 11 — Deload

Same exercises. Cut sets roughly in half (3→2, 4–5→2–3). Reduce load 10–20%. Stay at 3–4+ RIR — no failure, no PR attempts, no intensity techniques. Keep protein and sleep exactly as normal. Don't skip this — it resensitizes muscle receptors and clears systemic fatigue before Block 2. When the deload ends, resume unchanged exercises at 100% of your last pre-deload working weight — no extra reset needed.

BLOCK 2 — Weeks 12–21 (Deep Stretch & Peak Contraction)

Same structure and rules as Block 1, with these swaps only. Keep the primary strength compounds (RDL, Smith Squat, Bench Press, Incline DB Press) unchanged to preserve your progression tracking — only accessories rotate, to introduce novel fiber recruitment. After the Week 11 deload, resume every unchanged exercise at 100% of your Week 10 working weight — the deload already cleared the fatigue, so a further cut just wastes weeks climbing back. If the first session feels harder than the target RIR, drop 2.5–5% for that session only. Only the newly swapped exercises start conservatively (10–15% lighter than you'd guess), then rebuild via double progression.

WEEK 22 — Deload

Same rules as Week 11. Then continue to Block 3 (Week 23) — see Long-Term Periodization below. Unchanged exercises resume at 100% of your Week 21 weights; newly swapped exercises start 10–15% lighter.

Long-Term Periodization (the 4-block rotation)

The program is a loop of four 10-week blocks, each followed by a deload week. Blocks 1 and 2 are exactly what you've already seen. Blocks 3 and 4 are the same program with fresh accessory exercises, so after Week 22 you don't go back to Block 1 — you continue to Block 3. After Block 4, you go back to Block 1.

Rules

  • Switch blocks only right after a deload week. Never mid-block, unless something hurts or the equipment is unavailable (then use the nearest option from the same row).
  • Treat each new exercise as new: start 10–15% lighter, log it separately, and let double progression rebuild the numbers over 2–3 weeks. Exercises that don't change between blocks resume at 100% of their pre-deload weight.
  • If you start a cut, finish it using the exercises of the block you're in (with the Cutting tables), then resume that block. Don't switch blocks mid-cut.
  • Take an extra off-schedule deload whenever joints, sleep, or motivation demand it.

Optional, after a year or two: if a joint complains about the same main lift, swap it for one block (10–15% lighter, then rebuild): Incline DB Bench → Incline Barbell Bench; Smith Machine Squat → High-Bar Barbell Squat (needs a rack with safeties); Flat Barbell Bench → Flat DB Bench; RDL → DB RDL.

Nutrition & Recovery (non-negotiable for actual growth)

  • Calories: +200–300 kcal/day above maintenance (maintenance only if you're carrying extra body fat to lose first)
  • Protein: 1.6–2.2 g/kg bodyweight/day, spread across 3–5 meals
  • Carbs: most of your remaining calories, enough to fuel 5 sessions/week
  • Fat: at least 0.6–0.8 g/kg/day
  • Creatine monohydrate: 3–5 g/day, any time
  • Sleep: 7–9 hours
  • Steps: 7,000–10,000/day
  • Weigh-in trend: aim for \~0.25–0.5% bodyweight gain per week — faster is mostly fat

CUTTING PHASE — Same Program, Adjusted Volume

Keep the exact exercises, order, and rep ranges from whichever block you're in. Only volume, effort, and calories change. Use this table whenever you switch into a deficit — swap it back to the standard numbers once you return to a bulk.

Key differences from the bulk version:

  • Compound lifts (bench, squat, RDL, rows, pulldowns) keep their full set count — these protect the most muscle
  • Isolation work drops by roughly 1 set per exercise as fatigue accumulates
  • Isolation RIR moves from 0–1/failure to 1–2 RIR — don't grind every set to failure in a deficit
  • Compound RIR stays at 1–2 as normal
  • Rest periods stay long (don't rush sets to "sweat more" — cardio fatigue shouldn't limit muscular output)
  • Deloads come more often (every 5–6 weeks instead of every 8–10)

If fatigue piles up further: collapse to 4 days

If you're 6–8 weeks into the deficit and recovering poorly even with reduced volume, merge:

  • Day 1: Upper A + Push B compounds only (bench, row, one press variation, one pulldown, one triceps move)
  • Day 2: Lower A + Lower B compounds only (squat, RDL, one leg curl, one calf raise)
  • Day 3: Pull B as written
  • Day 4: Repeat Day 1 or 2 with reduced volume

Better to train 4 days well-recovered than 5 days half-recovered.

Cutting Phase Nutrition

  • Calories: –300 to –500 kcal/day below maintenance
  • Protein: 2.2–2.6 g/kg bodyweight/day (higher than the bulk target — this is what protects muscle)
  • Rate of loss: \~0.5–1% bodyweight/week — faster risks losing muscle along with fat
  • Cardio: 2–3 sessions/week, low-intensity (incline walk, cycling), kept away from your heaviest lower-body day if possible
  • Deload triggers: apply earlier and more readily than in a bulk — don't wait for the scheduled deload week if performance or sleep tanks sooner

Deload Early If:

  • Performance drops for 2+ weeks in a row
  • Joints ache persistently
  • Sleep quality tanks
  • Motivation collapses

Don't wait for the calendar — take the deload immediately.

Quick Troubleshooting

  • A muscle is lagging: add 2–3 sets/week to it, pull 2–3 sets from a muscle that's already growing well
  • A lift stalls 3+ weeks: check sleep and calories before touching the exercise
  • Joint pain: swap the movement pattern immediately — never train through sharp pain
  • Missed a session: skip it and carry on — never double up. Missed a week or more: repeat your last completed week at 5–10% lighter weights.

Appendix: Bulk vs. Cut FAQ

1. Will this program be helpful in a bulk phase?

Yes — this routine is built specifically for a bulk (hypertrophy) phase. During a caloric surplus, your recovery capacity, glycogen stores, and systemic energy are at their peak, and this program is designed to capitalize on that:

  • High mechanical tension: heavy, stable compounds (Smith squats, incline DB press, RDLs) drive progressive overload.
  • Solid volume (\~7–15 direct sets/muscle/week, plus indirect work from the compounds): right in the sweet spot for maximizing muscle protein synthesis when fuel is abundant.
  • Double progression: a clear rep target and weight-progression rule ensures the surplus calories go toward lean tissue rather than just body fat.

2. Can you follow the same program during a cut?

Yes — you should follow the same program. The most common mistake lifters make on a cut is switching to high-rep "toning" circuits with lighter weights.

  • The golden rule of fat loss: the stimulus required to build muscle is essentially the same stimulus required to retain it.
  • If you drop the weights or the mechanical tension, your body reads that as a signal that the muscle isn't needed — and it will happily burn it alongside body fat.

3. What changes are needed during a cutting phase?

The exercises, rep ranges, and loads stay identical. What changes is volume and fatigue management, because recovery capacity drops significantly in a deficit.

Variable Bulking Phase Cutting Phase Why the Change?
Working Sets 3 sets per exercise (baseline) Keep compounds at full sets; trim isolation work by \~1 set You need far less volume to maintain muscle than to build it (roughly 2/3 of bulking volume). Fewer sets means less accumulated fatigue.
Proximity to Failure (RIR) 0–1 RIR on isolations; occasional failure Strict 1–2 RIR across the board Training to true failure in a deficit spikes systemic fatigue without adding extra muscle retention.
Loads / Weights on the Bar Aim to increase weekly Fight to keep weights identical The goal on a cut is performance preservation — if you benched 80 kg for 8 reps and can still hit that, no muscle was lost.
Rest Intervals 2–3 min compounds; 60–90s isolations Keep rests long (2.5–3 min) Don't rush sets to "sweat more" — long rests keep cardiovascular fatigue from capping muscular output.
Deload Frequency Every 8–10 weeks Every 5–6 weeks Connective tissue recovers more slowly when glycogen, hydration, and calories are all reduced.

4. When to plan a bulk vs. a cut?

Rather than picking dates on a calendar, plan your phases around body fat level, training age, and how you're performing/recovering — not a fixed schedule.

   Lean Bulk (10–24+ Weeks)   ◄──────────  Bottom End (lower BF% range)

A. When to cut

  • Body fat trigger: roughly 16–20% for men, 24–28% for women — women naturally carry more essential fat, so their thresholds sit higher across the board, not just at this stage. Visually: waist circumference climbing, abs fully blurred, softness settling around the lower back/hips.
  • Performance signals: persistent sluggishness, achy joints, constant fullness/bloating, and gym pumps going flat are common signs of declining insulin sensitivity — often shows up before the mirror does.
  • Typical duration: 8–12 weeks. Keep the deficit brief and targeted — aim for \~0.5–1% bodyweight loss per week rather than dragging the cut out, which is what actually risks losing muscle.

B. When to bulk

  • Body fat floor: roughly 10–12% for men, 18–22% for women — faint abs visible in normal lighting, visible vascularity in the arms/forearms (men) or a lean, defined waistline (women).
  • Why start lean: nutrient partitioning is best when you're already lean — a larger share of surplus calories goes toward muscle protein synthesis instead of fat storage.
  • Duration depends on training age, not a fixed number:
  • Beginner/early intermediate (under \~2 years of consistent training): can run a longer bulk, 16–24+ weeks (4–6 months) — you're still adding muscle fast enough to justify the extended surplus.
  • Advanced or already fairly lean: shorter mini-bulks, 10–16 weeks, work better. Muscle gain slows down with training age while fat-gain risk doesn't, so a long surplus mostly just adds fat past a certain point.
  • Surplus: +200–300 kcal/day (\~0.25–0.5% bodyweight gain per week) regardless of which duration you're running.

C. Estimating body fat without lab equipment

  • Most people don't have DEXA/BodPod access, and that's fine — exact percentage matters far less than the trend. A monthly progress photo in the same lighting/pose, plus a tracked waist-circumference measurement, is a reliable enough proxy for deciding when to switch phases.
  • If you want a rough number, visual body-fat comparison charts (searchable online, showing photos at each 2–3% increment) get you close enough for this purpose — precision isn't the point, direction is.

D. The "maintenance buffer" (don't skip this) Never jump straight from a deficit into a hard surplus, or vice versa.

  • Spend 1–2 weeks at maintenance calories between phases — lean toward 2–4 weeks if the cut ran the full 12 weeks or longer.
  • This lets your metabolic rate, thyroid output, and hunger hormones (ghrelin/leptin) normalize, and prevents rapid fat rebound when you start the next bulk.
